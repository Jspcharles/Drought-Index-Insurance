1 Overview

The question

How well does a simple tiered contract, triggered by growing-season SPI-3, compensate wheat farmers for drought-driven yield losses, and where does it fail?

Why index insurance needs a drought index

Conventional (indemnity) crop insurance pays for losses measured on each farm. That needs loss assessment, which is slow and expensive, and it invites moral hazard and adverse selection. Index insurance instead pays out when an independently measured index crosses a trigger agreed in advance. Nobody visits the farm, payouts can be fast, and the insured cannot influence the index.

The price is basis risk: the index is not the farmer's loss. A farmer can lose a crop while the index stays above the trigger (a missed loss), or be paid in a year when the crop was fine (a false alarm). Choosing and designing the index is therefore the central research problem.

A standardised drought index such as the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) is attractive because the same value means the same rarity everywhere: a given SPI is equally unusual in a wet region and a dry one, and in any season. One set of trigger levels can then be applied across regions with very different climates.

Why synthetic data

With synthetic data the truth is known. Dry spells are injected at known dates, and the link between rainfall and yield is set by assumption, so each step can be checked against what it should find. The price is realism: see Limitations.

What makes the rainfall realistic

  • Seasonality: a cosine annual cycle, peaking in each region's wettest month.
  • Month-to-month variability: rainfall is drawn from a gamma distribution; a smaller shape k means more erratic rain.
  • Persistence: an AR(1) anomaly (lag-1 correlation \phi = ) makes a dry month likelier to be followed by another, as happens with ENSO or soil-moisture feedback.
  • Rainless months: in the dry half of the year a month can receive no rain at all. This matters for SPI, which has to handle zeros explicitly.

3 Methods

3.1 Synthetic rainfall

For region r, month t and calendar month m:

\mu_m = \frac{P}{12}\left[1 + a\cos\frac{2\pi(m - m_{\text{peak}})}{12}\right]
z_t = \phi\, z_{t-1} + \sqrt{1-\phi^2}\;\varepsilon_t,\qquad \varepsilon_t \sim N(0,1)
R_t \sim \text{Gamma}\!\left(k,\; \theta_t = \frac{\mu_m\, e^{\sigma z_t - \sigma^2/2}\, d_t}{k}\right), \qquad R_t = 0 \text{ with probability } p_0(m)

P, a, k and m_{\text{peak}} are the per-region values in the table above; \phi = , \sigma = . The multiplier e^{\sigma z - \sigma^2/2} has mean 1, so persistence changes the timing of wet and dry periods without changing mean rainfall. d_t is the dry-spell factor (1 outside spells), and p_0(m) is non-zero only in the dry half of the year.

3.2 Standardized Precipitation Index

Following McKee et al. (1993) and the WMO SPI User Guide (2012), computed for :

  1. Sum rainfall over the k months ending at t: X_t = \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} R_{t-i}.
  2. For each calendar month separately, fit a two-parameter gamma distribution G(x;\alpha,\beta) (location fixed at 0, maximum likelihood) to the non-zero totals in the calibration period (). Fitting by calendar month removes seasonality: a January total is only compared with other January totals.
  3. Convert each total to a cumulative probability with a mixed distribution that allows for zeros, then map it onto the standard normal:
H(x) = q + (1-q)\,G(x;\alpha,\beta), \qquad q = \frac{m}{n+1}
\text{SPI}_t = \Phi^{-1}\!\big(H(X_t)\big)

m of the n totals are zero. Zero rainfall: the gamma distribution is undefined at zero, so zero totals are given the centre of the zero-probability mass, H(0) = (m+1)/\big(2(n+1)\big), as Stagge et al. (2015) recommend. Using q itself would give rainless months a positive SPI wherever rainless months are common, which makes no physical sense. Probabilities are clipped to [p, 1-p] with p = , which caps SPI at ±. The first k-1 months have no SPI.

3.3 Drought events: run theory

An event (Yevjevich, 1967) is an unbroken run of months with < (shortest run counted, in months: ). For each run of D months:

S = \sum_{t \in \text{run}} \left(-\text{SPI}_t\right), \qquad I = \frac{S}{D}, \qquad \text{peak} = \min_{t \in \text{run}} \text{SPI}_t

Duration D, severity S (McKee's “drought magnitude”) and intensity I.

3.5 Insurance contract and evaluation

Index: the lowest SPI-3 over the windows ending in (), all inside the growing season. Taking the minimum means one dry quarter at any stage of the season can trigger a payout.

I_{r,y} = \min_{w \in W}\ \text{SPI-3}_{r,w,y}

Payout: tiered, as a share of the sum insured (SI). The sum insured is of expected yield, so payouts and losses are both expressed as a percentage of expected yield and can be compared directly.

Loss: as an analyst would, without knowing the true synthetic trend, fit a linear trend \hat T to each region's yield and measure the shortfall below it. A loss year has L > .

L_{r,y} = \max\!\left(0,\ \frac{\hat T_{r,y} - Y_{r,y}}{\hat T_{r,y}}\right)

Evaluation, from the 2×2 table of “paid?” against “loss year?”:

\text{hit rate} = \frac{\text{hits}}{\text{hits} + \text{misses}}, \qquad \text{false alarm ratio} = \frac{\text{false alarms}}{\text{hits} + \text{false alarms}}
\text{basis risk} = \sqrt{\tfrac{1}{N}\textstyle\sum \big(\text{payout} - L\big)^2} \quad\text{and}\quad \text{corr}(\text{payout}, L)

Hit rate and false alarm ratio only ask whether the contract paid. The RMSE and correlation also ask whether it paid the right amount.

6 Limitations

This is a practice workflow. Compared with real weather-index insurance research, it leaves out a lot:

  • Synthetic data. The strength of the rainfall–yield link (\beta_{\text{dry}} = ) and the size of the non-rainfall noise are set by assumption, not estimated. The results show how the method behaves, not how well SPI insurance would work anywhere real.
  • Everything is in-sample. SPI is fitted on the same years it is evaluated on, and the contract is judged on the data it was designed with. Real studies calibrate on one period and test on another, or cross-validate. With pooled loss years the hit rate is itself uncertain (95% interval –).
  • No spatial basis risk. Each region has one rainfall series that applies equally to every farm. In reality the index comes from a station or grid cell some distance away, and farm-level losses differ from regional averages.
  • No spatial correlation. Regions are generated independently. Real droughts are spatially correlated, and that correlation dominates an insurer's portfolio risk and reinsurance cost.
  • Rainfall only. No temperature, evapotranspiration (e.g. SPEI), soil moisture, remote sensing or crop-growth model. The index windows are fixed calendar months rather than crop stages.
  • Stationary climate. One gamma distribution per calendar month for the whole record assumes no climate trend; a drying climate would shift both the SPI baseline and the payout frequency.
  • Simple detrending and loss definition. A linear trend fitted to points is itself uncertain, especially with a dry spell near the end of the record, and a fixed threshold is only one possible definition of a loss.
  • No pricing or welfare. Payout tiers are illustrative, not priced. There is no premium, loading, or demand analysis, and no measure of farmer welfare such as certainty-equivalent income or expected utility, which is how the value of reducing basis risk is usually judged.
  • No data problems. Real station records have gaps, errors and changes of instrument; these series are complete and perfect by construction.
